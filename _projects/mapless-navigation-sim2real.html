Problem

Trained in simulation, driven on the robot.
The path is not the same.

Reinforcement learning policies for LiDAR-based mapless navigation are trained in simulation and deployed on real mobile robots. When the simulated dynamics differ from the real ones, the same velocity commands move the two robots differently, and the policy's performance degrades after deployment.

This work narrows the gap at its source. It fits the simulator's wheel–ground friction and suspension stiffness and damping to response data measured on the real robot, trains the same PPO policy once on the untuned and once on the tuned simulator, and checks on the robot whose real path stays closer to its simulated path.

What is measured. Not only whether the robot reaches the target, but how closely its real path, position and heading, follows the path simulation showed. The closer they are, the more what is seen in simulation says about what the robot will do.

Method

Fit the wheels to the robot, then train

  1. Measure the robot

    Wheel torque and angular velocity on the real robot, driving straight and turning in place, under six load cases.

  2. Tune the simulator

    Isaac Sim wheel–ground friction and suspension stiffness and damping, adjusted iteratively until

    r ≥ 0.9steady-state MAE ≤ 0.1mean of 6 cases

  3. Train two policies

    Identical PPO training in Isaac Lab, once on the untuned and once on the tuned simulator.

  4. Deploy and compare

    Both policies on the physical robot, two targets, five runs each. Each real path is compared with the policy's simulated path.

How the paths are compared

Aligned RMSE m

Both paths are resampled to 300 points by arc length and matched point to point with dynamic time warping (DTW). Root-mean-square distance over the matched pairs: where the robot was.

Normalized DTW m

The DTW distance divided by the number of matched pairs, that is, the mean distance between matched points: how alike the path shapes are.

Heading RMSE rad

Root-mean-square difference in heading over the same matched pairs: which way the robot faced along the path.

Significance: one-sided Welch's t-test and Mann–Whitney U test over the five runs per condition, α = 0.05.

  • By the LiDAR estimate, tuning reduced aligned RMSE by 17.9 %, normalized DTW by 17.5 % and heading RMSE by 24.3 %, averaged over both targets.
  • The reductions were significant on all three metrics at Target 2, and on aligned RMSE and heading RMSE at Target 1. Target 1's normalized DTW was not significant (p = 0.18).
  • The odometry estimate agreed in direction on every metric at both targets: 5.6 %, 8.2 % and 25.1 % on average.

Measurement

Two ways to measure where the robot went

A difference of a few centimetres means something only if the real path is measured well. Every comparison was therefore computed twice, from two estimates that fail in different ways.

LiDAR scan matching

Each 2D scan is registered to a map built from the run's earlier scans, by point-to-line ICP. No odometry enters the path, apart from one fixed mounting-angle correction estimated once for all recordings.

Wheel odometry

Integrated from the wheel encoders. Smooth, but it cannot see the wheels slip.

Limitations

  • Small sample. Two targets in one arena, five real runs per policy and target.
  • Not every metric is significant. At Target 1 the normalized DTW reduction (6.3 %) was not significant (p = 0.18).
  • The two estimates differ in size. By wheel odometry the position gains are smaller, 5.6 % and 8.2 % against 17.9 % and 17.5 % by LiDAR. Heading agrees.
  • One simulated path per condition. Each policy's five real runs are compared with a single rollout in simulation.
  • Two parameter groups. The tuning covers wheel–ground friction and suspension stiffness and damping only.
