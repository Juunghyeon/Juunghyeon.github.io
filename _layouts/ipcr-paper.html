The problem

Robot references come from nominal geometry, a CAD model or a taught plane, and the real surface differs. On contact, the reference is infeasible and the controller must move it somewhere. Many directions restore contact and regulate force equally well, yet they do not give the same result.

Take a circle whose position is fixed in an external frame, say to line up with a later assembly step. A typical contact controller moves the commanded point to the nearest point on the board and lets compliance along the normal take up the rest. Even when it is given the true normal, the circle lands up to 18 mm from its planned place, more than half its radius. The strokes finish, force is regulated and every tracking error is small, because errors are measured against the corrected point. A contact report does not reveal it.

Two levels, two ways to lose intent

The task names a yield subspace 𝒟, the directions in which motion may depart from the reference, and preserved coordinates \(y = Lx\) with \(LD = 0\). Drawing at a fixed position may yield in depth but keeps its frontal position; transport may yield sideways but keeps progress, height and possibly yaw. The nearest point is not wrong in this view: it is the right rule for a task with \(\mathcal{D}=\operatorname{span}(n)\), such as one defined on the surface.

Target level

The correction that restores feasibility must lie in 𝒟. Given an estimated constraint \(\hat A x = \hat b\), there is exactly one such point:

Theorem 1 · intent-preserving realization $$x_D^\star = r + D(\hat A D)^{-1}(\hat b - \hat A r), \qquad \hat A x_D^\star = \hat b,\quad L x_D^\star = L r .$$

An error in the surface estimate affects only whether \(x_D^\star\) is feasible, never \(Lx_D^\star\). Any other correction drifts by a principal-angle amount; for one plane violated by depth \(h\) the drift in the preserved coordinates is \(|h|\sin\theta\). This is a property of the rule, not of estimation error: it is there with the exact normal.

Theorem 2 · principal-angle drift $$\|x_E^\star - x_D^\star\|^2 = \sum_i (u_i^\top \bar e)^2 \tan^2\theta_i$$

Execution level

The servo then absorbs whatever residual \(\rho\) the imperfect estimate leaves. That absorption reaches the preserved coordinates by a computable law, and it vanishes for every residual if and only if the absorption direction lies in \(\ker L\):

Theorem 3 · execution leak $$\Delta(Lx) = G(\hat N)\,\rho, \qquad G(\hat N) = -L\hat N\,(A\hat N)^{-1}$$

The leak is a transfer times a residual, so there are two protections: shrink the residual, or absorb where the transfer vanishes. Our controller does both. From joint sensing alone it re-anchors its surface belief at contact, and it absorbs along 𝒟, with the absorption direction set separately from the physical force direction.

Results

The results in this section are from simulation (Gazebo and ROS 2 with a plant identified on the physical Dobot CR3A). Arms, metrics, decision rules and conditions were registered before each set of runs, and failed criteria are reported alongside the passes.

Same speed, same coverage, different placement

IPCR against the nearest point given the true normal, at 5 mm/s through both strokes on four boards. Both arms kept contact on 98–100 % of the figure, with no missing arc over 10°. Every registered criterion passed.

We crossed the target rule with the absorption direction, all with the true normal, and injected a residual by offsetting the believed plane by 10 mm. The leakage law, measured

Curved boards and a biased normal

Theorem 1 needs no planarity. With only a planar normal estimate, IPCR kept place and size on spheres of radius 150 and 250 mm and cylinders of 250 mm: shift 0.44–0.68 mm, scale 1.000–1.009. The nearest point given the true local normal moved the figure 15.5–21.9 mm on the spheres and the vertical cylinder. Rotating the normal estimate by ±3° moved the nearest-point figure by 8.2 mm and IPCR's by 0.27 mm, since IPCR's target does not use the normal for the preserved coordinates.

Same controller, different task

Changing only the task specification changed the realization as predicted. Over 108 runs (boards at 8.5°, 20° and 30°, friction 0 and 0.3, payloads 0.5–2.5 kg), the task “keep yaw” kept yaw within 1° in 54/54 runs and yielded a median 20.0 mm, the geometric requirement; “yaw free” turned in 54/54 runs (median −8.9°) and yielded less in 18/18 conditions. Limitations, stated plainly

  • Speed costs coverage. Holding the frontal motion exactly requires a depth velocity of up to v·tan θ. Our controller learns the slope late and corrects at a limited rate, so on steep boards at 10 mm/s it loses contact on part of the figure. Slowing down restores it. This is a limit of the implementation, not of the rule.
  • Force is regulated, not bounded. Peaks above 30 N occurred, and IPCR's were no lower than the nearest point's.
  • Absorption matters only when a residual persists. Residuals arising on their own were small on our boards; the difference between absorption directions was shown under injected offsets.
  • Scope. The guarantee covers the task-selected coordinates only and presumes a correctly specified (𝒟, L). A curved surface's local attitude is not tracked. On hardware we have so far run the pen task (circle on three boards and the absorption test); other shapes and box transport on hardware are pending.
